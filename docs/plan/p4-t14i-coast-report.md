# England's coast: stretches split by sea, short ones joined (P4-T14i)

Written 20 November 2026, from the Western Europe trial, rebuilt the same evening with the change below. Requirement: INF-2. Your note on 5.6 of the Phase 3 tidy-up 2 checklist.

## Summary

* England's coast now reads as four stretches and the coast of its islands, in place of one stretch of 4,070 kilometres named after the ocean and two short stretches with the same neighbour at both ends.
* The south coast reads as the Bristol Channel and then the English Channel. There is no Celtic Sea: the trial's Seas, bays and straits layer, from Natural Earth, has none, so the Cornish coast takes the Bristol Channel on the north side and the English Channel on the south.
* The two short stretches are gone: each is joined to the stretch beside it.
* Wales, Scotland and France read better too. One oddity in Wales comes from the seas layer, not from this change: see "Something left for later".

## Why England's coast read as it did

### The long stretch named after the ocean

* A stretch of coast runs between two neighbours. England's coast from the Severn, where it meets Wales, round Land's End and up the Channel and the east coast to the Tweed, where it meets Scotland, has no neighbour on the way, so it was one stretch of 4,070 kilometres.
* A stretch took the most detailed water touching at least half of it. The Bristol Channel, the English Channel and the North Sea each touch less than half of that stretch. Only the North Atlantic Ocean, which holds all the water, touches all of it, so the stretch was named after the ocean.
* So your first guess was right in part: it was not the sea of most of its length, but the only water touching most of it.

### The two short stretches

* Your guess of small breaks at the estuaries was close, but they are not breaks. The tidal water of the Dee and of the Tweed reaches up past the border, so the shore crosses the border back and forth.
* On the Dee, near Queensferry, the shore goes England, then 2.1 kilometres of Wales, then 3.3 kilometres of England, then Wales again. The 3.3 kilometres of England had Wales at both ends.
* On the Tweed, the border meets the river a few kilometres inland. The shore goes from England's south bank to 2.5 kilometres of Scotland's north bank, then 17 kilometres of England's: the north bank down through Berwick-upon-Tweed, round the river mouth and up the coast to Marshall Meadows, where the border meets the sea. Those 17 kilometres had Scotland at both ends.
* Wales and Scotland had the same in mirror image: 2.1 kilometres with England at both ends on the Dee, and 2.5 kilometres with England at both ends on the Tweed.

## The change

All in the coast-stretches build stage. Nothing in the application changed, and the wording is the wording you approved.

### Short stretches joined

* A stretch under 20 kilometres with the same neighbour at both ends is joined to the stretch beside it: the one with that neighbour at its nearest end, within 20 kilometres.
* The joined stretch's length is the two stretches' own lengths added together.
* A short stretch with no such stretch beside it stays as it is. Monaco's coast, for example, has France at both ends and is its only stretch.
* On the trial, 110 short stretches were joined this way, most of them counties'. 97 are left, each with no stretch beside it with the same neighbour, such as Monaco's.

### Stretches split where the sea changes

* Each step along a stretch takes the most detailed water touching it, such as the English Channel rather than the ocean.
* The stretch is split where that water changes.
* A run under 20 kilometres on another water, such as a small bay the named sea does not reach, goes to the longer run beside it, so a stretch is not cut into many small bits.
* Where a stretch is split, the new end has no neighbour. The application already says such a stretch as "Coast from Wales" or "Coast to Wales", or just "Coast" when neither end has a neighbour.

### What else it changed on the trial

* 1,637 stretches in all, up from 1,585: more by splitting, fewer by joining.
* Hampshire is unchanged: 83, 53 and 9.4 kilometres, all on the English Channel.
* Kent splits into the North Sea, 137 kilometres from Medway, and the English Channel, 64 kilometres to East Sussex.
* Cornwall splits into the Bristol Channel, 266 kilometres, and the English Channel, 527 kilometres, in place of one stretch of 792 kilometres with Devon at both ends.
* Northumberland's Berwick shore, 17 kilometres with the Scottish Borders at both ends, is joined to its stretch to North Tyneside, now 175 kilometres.
* The stage takes 51 seconds, as before. The rebuild ran only the coast-stretches, writing and checks stages, in 6 minutes 12 seconds, and all 60 check places passed.

## Something left for later

* In Wales, Milford Haven reads as the Irish Sea, between two stretches on the Bristol Channel. Both its ends lie exactly on 5 degrees west. The seas layer gives the water of Milford Haven east of 5 degrees west to the Irish Sea, because the build decides which sea a bay's water belongs to one grid square at a time. This is a fault in the seas layer, not in the stretches, and it would need its own task.
* The list goes west to east by each stretch's west end, so Wales's four stretches do not read in order round the coast. That is as you approved it.
* The meaning of "Stretch of coast" in the glossary says a stretch runs between two neighbours. It now can also end where one sea meets the next, so the glossary and the data manager's configuration notes need a line each.
